# Literature Review & Project Methodology

*Semantic-Augmented Detection of Wordlist-Based Domain Generation Algorithms*

This review establishes the positioning of this repository's work: what is known about wordlist DGA detection, which results are verified against their primary sources, and where the open gap is. It was written as part of this project and its verification notes are preserved deliberately.

---

## 1. Introduction

Domain Generation Algorithms (DGAs) allow malware to compute large numbers of candidate rendezvous domains algorithmically rather than relying on a fixed, hard-coded command-and-control address. This lets a botnet survive takedown of any single domain, since the operator and infected hosts can independently compute the next domain to use.

This review focuses on a specific, still-open sub-problem: domains generated by stitching together real dictionary words, known as **wordlist-based** or dictionary DGAs. Families such as Suppobox, Matsnu, and Gozi construct domains from natural-language vocabulary rather than pseudo-random characters, producing strings that read as far more plausible than typical DGA output — which lets them evade detectors built around character-level randomness.

## 2. DGA Taxonomy and the Scope of the Problem

Plohmann et al. (2016) conducted the foundational large-scale measurement study of real-world DGA families, analyzing 43 reverse-engineered implementations from DGArchive and formalizing the taxonomy still used today: arithmetic-based, hash-based, wordlist-based, and permutation-based generation. Their entropy analysis showed wordlist-based families have markedly lower character-level entropy than the other categories — the reason randomness-based classifiers struggle against them.

A consistent finding across the literature reviewed here is that wordlist-based DGAs are a small minority of real-world malware families, yet they receive disproportionate research attention because they represent the subset that defeats otherwise mature detection methods.

This motivates the project's scope: rather than general-purpose DGA classification (comparatively well solved for arithmetic/hash-based families), this work targets the specific, unsolved case of wordlist-based generalization to unseen families.

## 3. Character-Level and Feature-Based Detection

Zago, Gil Pérez and Martínez Pérez (2020) introduced UMUDGA, a large-scale dataset pairing 50 malware DGA families with benign domains, alongside a 49-dimension hand-crafted feature set (statistical + NLP-derived) for classification. Co-published across *Computers & Security* and *Data in Brief*, UMUDGA remains one of the most widely cited DGA benchmarks and is the primary dataset for this project.

> **Verification note:** the dataset's own data descriptor confirms domains are generated deterministically — each DGA is executed with a fixed random seed — rather than collected from live sinkhole traffic; worth stating explicitly in this project's limitations.

Lison and Mavroeidis (2017) trained a character-level GRU jointly across 61 malware families (one-hot character input, no pretraining or external linguistic knowledge) and reported a strong aggregate result (F1 = 0.971, 93% detection at a 1:100 false-positive rate). Buried inside that strong aggregate is an important negative result for the wordlist subset specifically: **Matsnu recall was only 0.158** in their per-family breakdown — one of the worst results in the entire 61-family table — which the authors attribute to Matsnu's dataset containing only 12,714 examples against a source vocabulary exceeding 1,300 words, i.e. not enough exposure for a from-scratch character model to learn the relevant vocabulary structure within a jointly-trained multi-family setup.

> **Verification note:** this is a joint multi-family model with a data-scarcity explanation for Matsnu specifically, not a dedicated single-family experiment. The same table shows Suppobox recall of only 0.125 with the character-bigram baseline, jumping to 0.931 with the neural model given sufficient examples — showing the wordlist problem is partly, but not entirely, a data-volume issue: given enough examples, a purely character-level model can eventually pick up wordlist structure, but Matsnu's small footprint meant it never got there.

## 4. Semantic and Pretrained-Knowledge Approaches

Koh and Rhodes (2018) (*Inline Detection of Domain Generation Algorithms with Context-Sensitive Word Embeddings*, IEEE Big Data 2018) took the complementary approach: instead of learning vocabulary from scratch, they fed a small fully-connected classifier (128 ReLU units + logistic regression) with **frozen, pretrained ELMo word embeddings**, loaded via TensorFlow Hub, unrelated to DGA data. Two preprocessing choices in their pipeline are directly relevant to this project's own design: they stripped the TLD before classification (finding it added no useful signal and made the model more robust to trivial TLD-swapping), and they word-segmented each domain label using a dynamic-programming, Zipfian-frequency-weighted segmenter (the `wordninja` package specifically) before embedding lookup.

Working with Matsnu, Rovnix, Pizd, and Suppobox, they showed the model reliably outperformed existing techniques with as few as 30 labeled examples per family and reached near-state-of-the-art with about 100. Their headline result, confirmed directly against the paper's results table: **a Matsnu classifier attained 89.5% detection rate at a 1:1,000 false-positive rate after 30 training examples, and 91.2% at a stricter 1:10,000 false-positive rate after 90 examples** — achieved in minutes of training. Critically, the authors verified this was not the model memorizing the wordlist: it correctly flagged Matsnu domains built from words never seen during training, indicating it had learned a semantic signature rather than a lookup table.

> **Positioning implication for this project:** Koh & Rhodes demonstrate the power of the semantic prior *with 30–90 labeled examples from the target family*. This project asks the strictly harder question — how far does that signal carry with **zero** exposure to the target family?

Cebere, Flueren, Sebastián, Plohmann and Rossow (2024) took a step back and meta-analyzed 38 published DGA detection papers against nine assumptions those papers implicitly rely on (e.g., a fixed number of samples being sufficient for any family, or generalist evaluation being representative of deployment). Their family-level analysis over DGArchive found that difficulty and the sample count needed for reliable detection vary enormously by family, and that many of the field's standard assumptions do not hold uniformly — an important methodological caution for how this project's own results should be interpreted and reported.

Leyva La O, Catania and Gonzalez (2026) extended the semantic-approach comparison to modern large pretrained models, benchmarking LLaMA 3.2, Gemma 3, ModernBERT, and several specialized baselines (DomBertUrl, LA_Bin07, CNN, FANCI) across **eight wordlist families used for training** and **three additional families held out** for generalization testing (bigviktor, ngioweb, pizd). ModernBERT achieved the strongest average performance — 86.7% F1 on trained families, **80.9% F1 on the three genuinely unseen families** (this project's ceiling anchor). The paper also reports that a domain-specialized ("expert") training approach beats a generalist model by 9.4% F1 on familiar variants and by 30.2% F1 on unseen families, arguing focused training develops transferable linguistic patterns rather than family-specific memorization.

> **Unverified in this pass:** an earlier draft stated Manuelita was the hardest family even when trained on directly, citing 43.1% F1. That specific per-family figure could not be re-located in the sources checked during revision (only the aggregate 86.7%/80.9% numbers were confirmed across multiple independent sources). Do not cite the 43.1% figure until pulled from the paper's own tables.

Read together, these studies form a coherent progression: character-only models trained from scratch fail on large-vocabulary wordlist families when per-family data is scarce, even in a joint multi-family setup (Lison & Mavroeidis); frozen pretrained word embeddings recover most of that lost performance with very little labeled data (Koh & Rhodes); and full pretrained transformers push in-family performance further, but generalization to a genuinely novel wordlist family remains inconsistent and family-dependent (Leyva La O et al.) — a pattern the meta-analysis (Cebere et al.) suggests should be expected rather than treated as an anomaly.

## 5. The Zero-Day Generalization Problem

The studies above mostly evaluate models against wordlist families they were, in some form, exposed to. The more operationally realistic scenario is a family the model has never encountered at all. Drichel, von Brandt and Meyer (2022) established the standard reference point for this setting: a context-free, leave-one-group-out evaluation across real-world DGA families from DGArchive (ARES 2022). Their best-performing approach (Regex–Error Detection) achieves a **median leave-one-cluster-out F1-score of 19.444%** — the exact, confirmed source of this project's zero-day floor benchmark.

The paper further explores class-incremental learning strategies for adapting an existing classifier as new families are discovered — underscoring that this is treated as an ongoing operational problem, not a one-off benchmark.

This zero-day setting, rather than in-family accuracy, is the specific gap this project's Leave-Families-Out protocol is designed to probe.

## 7. Gap in the Literature and Project Positioning

Three gaps motivate this project's design:

- **No study reviewed here evaluates a dual-branch architecture** (explicit character-level branch plus explicit frozen semantic/word-level branch, trained jointly at the fusion layer only) under a leave-families-out protocol specifically on wordlist DGAs. Prior semantic approaches either replace the character branch entirely (Koh & Rhodes — embeddings only) or rely on a single large pretrained transformer without an explicit separate lexical branch (Leyva La O et al.).
- **Published zero-day baselines (Drichel et al.) are computed across all DGA families**, not isolated to the wordlist subset — it is hard to know how much of that floor is attributable to wordlist families specifically.
- **The 80.9%/84.6% "unseen" ceiling of Leyva La O et al. was measured on bigviktor, ngioweb, and pizd** — the three families that paper held out. Matsnu and Manuelita were part of its *training* set. This matters directly for how this project's split is designed and how its results can be honestly compared against that ceiling.

Building on this foundation, the project trains a dual-branch frozen-FastText + BiLSTM model on a mixed pool of wordlist and non-wordlist families, supplemented with five additional wordlist families, and evaluates under a Leave-Families-Out protocol with Matsnu, Ngioweb, and Pizd held out entirely as final, single-use test families (Manuelita is evaluated as a validation-time diagnostic instead).

## 8. Project Methodology (design decisions)

### 8.1 Datasets and data merging
UMUDGA (Mendeley DOI 10.17632/y8ph45msv8.1) supplies all 26 non-wordlist training families plus 7 wordlist/wordlist-adjacent families; the Hugging Face release supplies the 5 families absent from UMUDGA (charbot, deception, manuelita, bigviktor, ngioweb). Multi-variant families (e.g. `gozi_gpl/luther/nasa/rfc4343`, `vawtrak_v1/v2/v3`) are collapsed to a single family label for split assignment, so no variant of a family can appear in both train and a held-out split. Full details: [`DATASETS.md`](DATASETS.md).

### 8.2 Wordlist vs non-wordlist classification
Wordlist status follows the UMUDGA paper's † markings, supplemented by its collision analysis (nymaim, pizd wordlist-adjacent), banjori retained as a disputed/mixed case in training only, and charbot/deception/manuelita/bigviktor/ngioweb as wordlist per Leyva La O et al. (2026).

### 8.3 Train / Val-Unseen / Test-Unseen split
Three-way family-level split (table in [`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md) §2). Two deliberate changes from an earlier draft:

- **`pizd` moved train → test-unseen.** It is one of only three families the Leyva La O et al. ceiling was actually measured against; leaving it in training would have burned the one clean apples-to-apples comparison point available for that number.
- **`manuelita` moved test-unseen → val-unseen.** It is a known-difficult wordlist family; keeping it invisible until the single final test pass meant discovering a possible blind spot only after there was no time left to respond. Evaluating it during validation gives an honest, actionable read on the hardest case while training is still adjustable — at the acknowledged cost of not being able to report a clean, one-shot blind score on it.

An earlier draft held out *every* wordlist family from training; that version was rejected because it would have let the model learn the shortcut "word-like structure = benign" rather than a genuine detection signal, since benign domains are also word-like.

### 8.4 Sample-count strategy
Train ≤ 20k/family (prevents 1M+-domain UMUDGA families from dominating gradients); val/test 2k/family (stable epoch metrics; matches the ceiling study's convention); benign 1:1 per split. Supply caveats stated plainly: bigviktor and ngioweb have only ~2,000 available samples (no headroom), and the 26-vs-6 family-count split means an 80/20 volume imbalance — addressed at training time via stratified batch sampling, not by changing caps.

### 8.5 Architecture rationale
- **Branch A (character-level):** BiLSTM over raw domain characters — catches high-entropy/algorithmic patterns. A from-scratch transformer was considered and rejected: with no large-scale pretraining it would likely underperform both the BiLSTM and the pretrained-ModernBERT baseline.
- **Branch B (semantic/word-level):** frozen pretrained FastText embeddings over DP-segmented substrings, following Koh & Rhodes's design: a frozen embedding forces the model to keep using general lexical-semantic structure rather than letting gradient descent find a cheaper character-level statistical shortcut. FastText replaces ELMo as lighter-weight and non-contextual, better suited to short domain strings; the `.bin` format (not `.vec`) gives subword-composed vectors for segmentation mistakes/rare tokens.
- **Fusion:** learnable gate (attention fusion considered as an ablation, not a baseline requirement).
- **ModernBERT fine-tune on this project's exact split** — because the published 86.7%/80.9% numbers were measured on a different split; any claim about matching or exceeding ModernBERT requires training it on the same data this project's models see.

### 8.6 Training procedure
Stratified batch sampler (~1:1 WL:NWL per batch); per-epoch evaluation on val-unseen (forward only); checkpoint on wordlist-family val macro-recall (not a blended aggregate — a blended metric could reward a checkpoint whose wordlist performance stagnates); non-wordlist tracked as guardrail; test-unseen evaluated exactly once after freezing.

## 9. Known Limitations

- Not a comprehensive leave-one-family-out sweep across all real-world DGA families — a targeted evaluation of wordlist generalization specifically.
- Only 3 truly held-out wordlist test families — stronger than single-family, still small; per-family results are case studies, not a statistically robust average.
- Manuelita is a validation-time diagnostic, not part of the blind final test — a deliberate trade-off, stated explicitly rather than left implicit.
- Banjori's wordlist classification is disputed; retained in training as a borderline case.
- Charbot is an adversarially generated family (designed to evade classifiers), included in training for robustness; flagged for transparency.
- The comparison against the 80.9–84.6% ceiling is only apples-to-apples for ngioweb and pizd. Matsnu is reported under a strictly harder condition than that study attempted (they trained on it).
- UMUDGA's domains are deterministically generated, not observed in live sinkhole or passive-DNS traffic — realistic strings, but registration timing and other real-world signals aren't represented.
- The benign/legit set is drawn from a top-domains-style list — a known "easy mode" critique; real-world deployment faces harder negatives.
- Strong performance here evidences wordlist-family generalization within this threat model, not a general robustness guarantee — recent work evaluating detectors against evolving real-world campaigns found models trained on standard corpora generalize poorly to newer tactics blending dictionary words with brand tokens and light randomization.
- The Manuelita 43.1%-F1 figure from an earlier draft could not be re-verified and must not be cited without primary-source confirmation.
